Shenoute of Atripe led the White Monastery for something close to eighty
years and left the largest surviving corpus of literature composed in
Coptic rather than translated into it. It is also, for a beginner, close
to unreadable — and the reason is codicology, not language.

## The library was taken apart

The White Monastery library survived into the modern period and was then
dismembered by the antiquities trade. Single codices were split leaf by
leaf and sold to different buyers, so the pages of one work now sit in
Paris, Naples, Vienna, London, Leiden and a dozen other collections. For a
long time nobody could say with confidence which leaves belonged to which
book.

Stephen Emmel's *Shenoute's Literary Corpus* is the work that put it back
together on paper: it reconstructs the original codices and gives the
corpus its shape — nine volumes of *Canons*, addressed to the monastic
community, and eight of *Discourses*, addressed outward. Citations you
meet in the literature assume that framework. Learn it before you learn
anything else about him.

## Then the language

Shenoute writes a Sahidic that is grammatically standard and rhetorically
extremely long-winded. Sentences run for a page. He quotes scripture
constantly and rarely marks where the quotation stops.

What works, in order:

1. Start with a short discourse rather than anything from the *Canons* —
   the *Canons* assume you know the monastery's internal disputes.
2. Keep Crum's *Coptic Dictionary* open. The
   [online edition](https://coptic-dictionary.org) is faster to search and
   good enough for reading.
3. Read a translation alongside on first pass. There is no prize for
   suffering.

---

The corpus is being edited and digitised piecemeal;
[Coptic Scriptorium](https://copticscriptorium.org) has a growing set of
annotated texts, and [PAThs](https://atlas.paths-erc.eu) maps the
manuscripts to the places they came from. Neither is complete. Both are
more than existed twenty years ago.
